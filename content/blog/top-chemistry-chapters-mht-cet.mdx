Chemistry is often the subject where students leave the most marks on the table, simply because the syllabus is wide, spans three genuinely different kinds of thinking, and it is honestly hard to know where to focus in the final weeks before the exam without a data-backed starting point. Physical, Inorganic, and Organic Chemistry each demand a different kind of preparation, and treating all three the same way is one of the quieter reasons students plateau in this subject even after putting in real study hours.

This guide covers the ten chapters that show up most often across MHT CET shifts, based on an analysis of past papers, organised by the three broad divisions of the syllabus, along with the methodology behind the ranking, a study approach tailored to each division, the specific habits that make these chapters feel harder than they actually are, and a realistic four-week revision schedule for using this list in the final month before the exam.

Chemistry differs from Physics and Maths in one important way worth keeping in mind throughout this guide: its three divisions genuinely reward different study skills. A student can be genuinely excellent at the numerical fluency Physical Chemistry demands while still struggling with the sustained factual recall Inorganic Chemistry requires, or the reverse, strong recall paired with slow, hesitant numericals. Recognising which kind of weakness you actually have, rather than treating "I am weak in Chemistry" as one undifferentiated problem, is often the single biggest unlock available in this subject.

## How This Ranking Was Built

A weightage claim is only useful if you trust where it came from, so it is worth being explicit about the methodology behind this list before relying on it for your own study planning.

### The Data Behind the Ranking

This ranking comes from tagging every Chemistry question across several years of MHT CET shifts by chapter, then counting frequency of appearance per chapter across that full window of shifts rather than relying on any single year's paper alone. A single year can be an outlier, for reasons as simple as which specific question-setter was assigned to that particular shift, so aggregating across multiple years produces a far more reliable signal about genuine, sustained weightage than any one paper could on its own.

### Why Ten Chapters and Not More

Chemistry's syllabus contains far more than ten chapters, and plenty of chapters outside this list still appear on the exam with real, non-trivial frequency. Ten was chosen as a practical cutoff: these are the chapters where the weightage gap between them and the rest of the syllabus is large enough to justify materially different prioritisation, rather than a marginal difference that would not actually change how you allocate study time. Chapters just outside this top ten still deserve solid coverage, and a light revision pass on each of them is still worthwhile time. They simply do not carry quite the same urgency, or the same expected return per hour of study, as the ten chapters covered specifically in this guide.

## Physical Chemistry: The Numerical Backbone

Physical Chemistry rewards formula fluency and calculation speed more than any other division of the syllabus, and three chapters within it account for a disproportionate share of the marks available.

### 1. Chemical Kinetics

Rate laws, order of reaction determined from experimental data, and the Arrhenius equation appear in nearly every shift, which makes Chemical Kinetics one of the most reliably tested chapters in the entire Chemistry syllabus. Questions here are highly formulaic once the underlying logic clicks: identify what the question gives you, whether that is concentration-time data or a rate constant at two different temperatures, and apply the corresponding standard equation. Students who struggle with this chapter are usually struggling with distinguishing between zero, first, and second order reactions from data rather than with the formulas themselves, so drilling that specific distinction pays off disproportionately.

### 2. Electrochemistry

Nernst equation applications, cell EMF calculations, and conductivity questions are frequent, and they are also some of the highest-scoring questions on the entire paper once the formulas are properly memorised. Electrochemistry rewards students who are comfortable moving between standard electrode potentials, cell notation, and the Nernst equation without hesitation, since a typical question chains two or three of these steps together. Building fluency here through repeated numerical practice, rather than just reviewing the theory, is what actually moves accuracy.

### 3. Solutions

Colligative properties, Raoult's Law, and questions involving molality and molarity conversions are common, and they pair well with numerical practice since the underlying formulas are predictable and repeat closely from year to year. This chapter is also one of the more forgiving ones in Physical Chemistry, in the sense that once you have solved a representative set of question types, unfamiliar variations are rare, which makes it a strong candidate for an early, confidence-building drill session.

### 4. Thermodynamics

Enthalpy, entropy, and Gibbs free energy calculations round out the Physical Chemistry cluster with moderate but consistent weightage across shifts. Thermodynamics questions on MHT CET tend to be more direct than their JEE-style equivalents, testing whether you can correctly apply a standard formula to a given process rather than requiring you to identify a non-obvious multi-step approach. A single well-organised reference sheet covering enthalpy of reaction, entropy change, and the relationship between Gibbs free energy and spontaneity covers the large majority of what gets tested here.

## Inorganic Chemistry: Fact-Dense and High-Yield

Inorganic Chemistry is less about formulas and more about facts, trends, and patterns, which means it needs a fundamentally different study approach from Physical Chemistry even though both divisions carry similarly high weightage.

### 5. p-Block Elements

Group 15 through 18 chemistry, especially nitrogen and halogen compounds, is one of the most heavily weighted chapters in all of Inorganic Chemistry, with questions spanning group trends, specific compound properties, and reaction-based questions across the groups. This chapter rewards students who can recall trends confidently under time pressure, atomic radius, electronegativity, oxidation states, rather than students who understand the trends conceptually but need time to reconstruct them from first principles during the exam.

### 6. d and f Block Elements

Transition metal properties and coordination compound basics appear consistently, particularly questions built around colour, magnetic properties, and oxidation state variability. This chapter shares a preparation challenge with p-Block Elements: the content is dense and fact-heavy rather than formula-driven, so the same recall-under-pressure skill matters more here than deep conceptual reasoning.

### 7. Coordination Compounds

Naming conventions, isomerism, and bonding theory questions show up often enough that this chapter deserves dedicated, standalone chapter-mode practice rather than being treated as an extension of d and f Block Elements, even though the two are closely related. IUPAC nomenclature rules and the different types of isomerism, structural and stereo, are the two sub-areas where students most often lose marks, usually from rushing rather than from not knowing the underlying rules.

## Organic Chemistry: Reaction Mechanisms and Reagents

Organic Chemistry forms the backbone of the final third of the Chemistry syllabus, and it rewards a specific kind of active, prediction-based practice that differs from both Physical and Inorganic Chemistry.

### 8. Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers

A high-frequency chapter with reliable reaction-mechanism questions, testing everything from preparation methods to characteristic reactions that distinguish alcohols, phenols, and ethers from one another. Questions here frequently test whether you can identify the correct reagent for a given transformation, which makes reagent recall as important as mechanism understanding.

### 9. Aldehydes, Ketones and Carboxylic Acids

Along with the previous chapter, this forms the backbone of organic reaction questions on the exam, covering nucleophilic addition reactions, oxidation and reduction relationships between the three functional groups, and characteristic tests used to distinguish them. This chapter benefits enormously from active practice: predicting a reaction's product before checking the answer, rather than passively reading through a worked example.

### 10. Amines

Basicity comparisons, both within amines and against related compounds, and standard preparation methods are common question types here. Amines is a chapter where a small number of recurring comparison questions, ranking basicity of different substituted amines, account for a large share of what actually gets tested, which makes targeted practice on exactly that comparison skill unusually efficient.

## What Makes These Ten Chapters Feel Harder Than They Are

A recurring pattern across all ten chapters is that the underlying content is more learnable than it initially feels, and the actual difficulty on exam day usually comes from a small number of specific, fixable habits rather than from the chapters being inherently hard.

### The Physical Chemistry Trap: Formula Lookup Under Pressure

In Kinetics, Electrochemistry, Solutions, and Thermodynamics, the formulas themselves are rarely the problem. What actually costs marks is hesitation: pausing mid-question to recall which specific variant of the Nernst equation applies, or whether a given Solutions question wants molality or molarity, and losing ten or fifteen seconds to that hesitation on every single numerical. Multiplied across a full paper, that hesitation adds up to real, avoidable time loss. The fix is not learning new formulas, it is timed repetition on the formulas you already know, until recall becomes automatic rather than something you have to consciously search for.

### The Inorganic Chemistry Trap: Confusing Similar Trends

In p-Block Elements, d and f Block Elements, and Coordination Compounds, the most common source of errors is confusing two genuinely similar trends, for example mixing up how a property changes down a group versus across a period, or misremembering which specific transition metal ion produces which colour. These are not conceptual failures so much as memory-interference problems, and they respond specifically well to spaced, active-recall practice rather than passive re-reading, since re-reading tends to reinforce the confusion rather than resolve it.

### The Organic Chemistry Trap: Recognising a Reaction Out of Context

In Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers, Aldehydes, Ketones and Carboxylic Acids, and Amines, a reaction that is instantly recognisable when it appears in isolation, in the chapter you are currently studying, can become genuinely difficult to recognise when it shows up unlabelled inside a mixed paper alongside questions from five other chapters. This is why chapter-mode drilling alone is not enough for Organic Chemistry specifically. Once you can solve a chapter's questions confidently in isolation, mixing that chapter into a broader Organic Chemistry set, without a chapter label telling you what to expect, is the step that actually prepares you for how these questions appear on the real exam.

## How to Study Each Cluster Differently

Treating all ten chapters with the same study method is one of the quieter reasons students under-perform in Chemistry relative to their actual effort. Each of the three clusters above responds best to a different approach.

### Physical Chemistry: Build One Reference Formula Sheet

Physical Chemistry rewards students who build a single, consolidated reference sheet covering every formula used across Kinetics, Electrochemistry, Solutions, and Thermodynamics, since the four chapters share enough numerical structure that cross-chapter confusion, mixing up which formula applies to which chapter, is a more common source of errors than genuine lack of knowledge. Reviewing that sheet immediately before a chapter-mode test, rather than trying to recall formulas cold under pressure, is a simple habit that measurably improves accuracy across this entire cluster.

### Inorganic Chemistry: Spaced Repetition Over Cramming

Because Inorganic Chemistry is fact-dense rather than logic-dense, short and frequent review sessions consistently beat long single sittings, particularly close to the exam when the temptation is to do one long revision pass the night before. A fifteen-minute active-recall pass through group trends or transition metal properties every couple of days, tested with a small chapter-mode set at the end of each week, retains far better than an equivalent amount of time spent in a single long session.

### Organic Chemistry: Predict Before You Check

Organic Chemistry rewards students who build a personal reference sheet of named reactions and common reagents, and who actively predict the product of a reaction before checking the answer key rather than passively recognising it once it is shown to them. This active-prediction habit is what actually builds the pattern-matching speed the exam demands under time pressure, since recognising a familiar reaction on sight is a different, faster skill than working it out from scratch, and only repeated active prediction builds it.

## Turning This List Into a Study Plan

Knowing which ten chapters matter most is the easy part. Turning that knowledge into a study plan that actually improves your Chemistry score takes a few deliberate steps.

### Step 1: Audit Your Accuracy Chapter by Chapter

Before prioritising, take a chapter-mode test across each of the ten chapters listed above, or a full Chemistry-only paper, and record your accuracy per chapter rather than just an overall Chemistry score. A student scoring well overall can still be losing most of their available marks in one or two of these ten chapters specifically, and that gap will not show up anywhere in an aggregate percentage.

### Step 2: Prioritise by Weightage and Weakness

Cross-reference your chapter-level accuracy against the weightage ranking in this guide. Chapters that are both high-weightage and low-accuracy for you personally are your highest priority, since every hour spent there produces the largest expected improvement in your score. Chapters that are high-weightage but already high-accuracy need only a light maintenance pass, not further deep study, freeing up time for the chapters that actually need it. Low-weightage chapters where you are already accurate need essentially no further attention until much closer to the exam, if at all.

### Step 3: Drill and Confirm

Run focused chapter-mode drills on your highest-priority chapters, using the cluster-specific study approach described above, formula-sheet review for Physical Chemistry, spaced recall for Inorganic Chemistry, and active prediction for Organic Chemistry. After each drilling session, confirm the improvement with a fresh attempt a few days later rather than assuming the work is done, and move to the next priority chapter only once the accuracy gain is confirmed with real data from that fresh attempt, not just a subjective sense that the chapter feels more familiar than it did before.

## A Sample Four-Week Chemistry Revision Schedule

Here is how a focused four-week Chemistry revision window, built around this list, might look in practice.

### Week One and Two

Run the accuracy audit across all ten chapters and begin drilling your three lowest-accuracy, highest-weightage chapters, one at a time, using the cluster-appropriate study method for each. By the end of week two, you should have a clear, confirmed improvement on at least the first of these three chapters, with the second and third following close behind as drilling continues into the following days.

### Week Three

Move to the second tier of priority chapters, typically two or three more chapters from the list that scored moderately in your original audit, while running a light maintenance pass, a short spaced-recall session or a quick formula sheet review, on the chapters you already drilled in weeks one and two, to make sure those earlier gains are actually holding rather than quietly fading as your attention moves elsewhere.

### Week Four

Attempt at least one full Chemistry-only paper under real timed conditions, review the results against your original audit to confirm overall progress across all ten chapters, and spend the final few days on light, low-intensity revision only, one focused pass over your formula and reagent reference sheets rather than new practice. Avoid introducing any chapter you have not already covered this late in the cycle, since the risk of shaky, half-formed knowledge going into the exam usually outweighs the benefit of last-minute coverage.

## Common Mistakes Students Make With This List

A few patterns show up repeatedly among students who discover chapter-weightage data for the first time.

### Treating the List as a Substitute for the Rest of the Syllabus

These ten chapters carry high weightage, but high weightage is not the same as exclusive weightage. Chapters outside this list still appear on the exam, and a strategy of studying only these ten chapters while ignoring the rest of the Chemistry syllabus entirely leaves real marks on the table. Use this list to prioritise your remaining time, not to define the boundary of what you study.

### Studying Organic Chemistry Without a Reagent Reference Sheet

Organic Chemistry questions frequently hinge on correctly identifying a specific reagent for a specific transformation, and students who study mechanisms conceptually without building a concrete, memorised reagent reference lose marks on exactly this kind of recall question, even when their conceptual understanding of the mechanism itself is genuinely solid.

### Ignoring Physical Chemistry's Shared Formula Structure

Students who study Kinetics, Electrochemistry, Solutions, and Thermodynamics as four entirely separate topics, rather than noticing the numerical structure they share, tend to take longer to identify the correct formula under exam time pressure than students who have consciously built the cross-chapter reference sheet described earlier in this guide.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does this weightage ranking change from year to year

The broad ranking is stable, since it reflects the underlying syllabus emphasis rather than any single year's specific paper-setting choices, but small shifts do happen. Revisit weightage data periodically through your preparation rather than treating this list as fixed forever, particularly if you are preparing many months ahead of your actual exam date.

### Should I study these ten chapters before anything else in Chemistry

Not necessarily before anything else, but with more time and repetition allocated to them relative to lower-weightage chapters. An efficient sequence is to get a first pass through the entire Chemistry syllabus for basic familiarity, then allocate the bulk of your deep, repeated practice time to these ten chapters specifically once you know your own chapter-level accuracy from an audit.

### I am weak in Organic Chemistry overall. Where should I start

Start with Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers and Aldehydes, Ketones and Carboxylic Acids, since they carry the highest weightage within Organic Chemistry and share enough reaction logic that improving one tends to improve your comfort with the other. Build your reagent reference sheet alongside this drilling rather than as a separate task, so the reference sheet grows directly out of the mistakes you are actually making.

### How is this list different from the general MHT CET chapter weightage guide on this site

This guide focuses specifically and only on Chemistry, going deeper into study methods tailored to each of the three Chemistry divisions than a broader guide covering all three subjects can. If you are looking for the equivalent weightage breakdown for Physics and Maths alongside Chemistry, the site's complete chapter weightage guide covers all three subjects together with a shared study-planning framework.

### Do these ten chapters apply equally to PCM and PCB students

Yes. Chemistry weightage patterns described in this guide apply equally to both PCM and PCB aspirants, since Chemistry is common to both streams and the exam does not distinguish between them for this subject specifically.

### I have limited time. Should I skip the chapters not on this list entirely

No. Skipping them entirely is a riskier trade than it looks, since even lower-weightage chapters still produce questions most years, and a chapter you have never touched at all is far more likely to cost you a question than one you gave even a light, single-pass review. If time is genuinely short, give every remaining chapter at least one lighter revision pass while reserving your deepest, most repeated practice for the ten chapters covered in this guide, rather than cutting the rest of the syllabus out completely.

### Which of these ten chapters is the best starting point if I am beginning Chemistry revision from scratch

Chemical Kinetics is a reasonable default starting point for most students, since it is high-weightage, strongly formula-driven, and its logic transfers directly into building comfort with Electrochemistry immediately afterward. Starting with a formula-driven Physical Chemistry chapter also tends to build early confidence before moving into the more fact-dense demands of Inorganic Chemistry, though your own accuracy audit, once you have one, should always take priority over this general default.

## Putting This Into Practice

Start with a single chapter-mode test on whichever of these ten chapters you suspect is your weakest, confirm that suspicion with real accuracy data rather than a gut feeling, and apply the cluster-appropriate study method, formula sheet, spaced recall, or active prediction, described in this guide. That one focused session, repeated across your remaining priority chapters over the following weeks, is the fastest way to turn this weightage list into an actual improvement in your Chemistry score.

Chemistry rewards consistency more visibly than either of the other two subjects, since its three divisions each compound in slightly different ways: formula fluency in Physical Chemistry, retention in Inorganic Chemistry, and pattern recognition in Organic Chemistry all improve fastest through short, frequent, focused sessions rather than occasional long ones. Treat this list as the starting map, not the whole journey, and let your own chapter-by-chapter accuracy data steer the rest.
