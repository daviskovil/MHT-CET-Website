Physics is usually the subject students either love or quietly dread, and the gap between the two often comes down to one thing that has little to do with raw talent: whether you know which chapters are actually worth your limited revision hours. The Physics syllabus spans mechanics, electricity, magnetism, modern physics, and optics, and treating all of it as equally important is one of the most common ways students burn weeks of study time without moving their score.

This guide covers the ten Physics chapters that show up most often across MHT CET shifts, based on an analysis of past papers, organised by the three natural clusters they fall into, along with the methodology behind the ranking, the specific habits that make these chapters feel harder than they actually are, a study approach tailored to each cluster, and a realistic four-week revision schedule for using this list in the final month before the exam.

## How This Ranking Was Built

A weightage claim is only useful if you trust where it came from, so it is worth being explicit about the methodology before relying on it for your own study planning.

### The Data Behind the Ranking

This ranking comes from tagging every Physics question across several years of MHT CET shifts by chapter, then counting frequency of appearance per chapter across that full window of shifts rather than relying on any single year's paper alone. A single year can be an outlier, for reasons as simple as which specific question-setter was assigned to that particular shift, so aggregating across multiple years produces a far more reliable signal about genuine, sustained weightage than any one paper could on its own.

### Why Ten Chapters and Not More

Physics contains far more than ten chapters, and plenty of chapters outside this list still appear on the exam with real, non-trivial frequency. Ten was chosen as a practical cutoff: these are the chapters where the weightage gap between them and the rest of the syllabus is large enough to justify materially different prioritisation, rather than a marginal difference that would not actually change how you allocate study time. Chapters just outside this top ten still deserve solid coverage, and a light revision pass on each of them is still worthwhile time. They simply do not carry quite the same urgency, or the same expected return per hour of study, as the ten chapters covered specifically in this guide.

## The Mechanics Cluster: The Numerical Backbone

Mechanics is where weightage skew is most pronounced in the entire Physics syllabus. Three chapters here share a common thread: they lean heavily on a small set of standard formulas applied to slightly varied setups, which makes them attractive for question-setters who want problems that are rigorous but not ambiguous.

### 1. Rotational Dynamics

Rotational Dynamics consistently accounts for the largest share of Physics questions across every year analysed, making it the single highest-priority chapter on this entire list for most students. It tends to produce numerical-heavy questions built around moment of inertia, angular momentum conservation, and rolling motion. Students who are comfortable with the standard derivations, but who have not practised enough varied numericals, are the ones who lose marks here, not because the concept is unfamiliar but because the specific combination in the question is new.

### 2. Circular Motion

Circular Motion questions are usually shorter and more direct than Rotational Dynamics questions, often testing banking of roads, conical pendulum setups, or vertical circular motion. This chapter rewards students who have memorised the handful of standard result formulas rather than re-deriving them under time pressure, since re-derivation under a strict per-question time budget is exactly where marks get lost.

### 3. Gravitation

Gravitation rounds out the mechanics cluster with questions on orbital velocity, escape velocity, and Kepler's laws. It is one of the more forgiving chapters in the sense that the question patterns repeat closely from year to year, which makes it a strong candidate for an early, confidence-building drill session before moving into the less predictable chapters elsewhere in the syllabus.

## The Electricity and Magnetism Cluster

Electricity and magnetism follow mechanics closely in overall frequency, and this cluster is where circuit-drawing and field-direction errors, rather than genuine conceptual gaps, tend to cost the most marks.

### 4. Electrostatics

Electrostatics questions cluster around electric field and potential due to standard charge configurations, capacitor combinations, and Gauss's law applications. The chapter is formula-dense, and the main challenge is correctly identifying which configuration a question is describing before applying the right formula, rather than the formulas themselves being difficult.

### 5. Current Electricity

Current Electricity leans on Kirchhoff's laws, Wheatstone bridge variants, and combination-of-resistors problems that are computationally straightforward once the circuit is correctly drawn out. Circuit-drawing errors are the main source of lost marks in this chapter, which is itself the main source of error under time pressure rather than any genuine gap in understanding the underlying physics.

### 6. Magnetic Effects of Current

Magnetic Effects of Current appears slightly less frequently than Electrostatics and Current Electricity but still often enough that it cannot be treated as optional, particularly for students aiming for the top percentile bands where every mark matters. Questions here typically test the force on a current-carrying conductor, the magnetic field due to standard current configurations, and torque on a current loop.

### 7. Electromagnetic Induction

Electromagnetic Induction rounds out this cluster with questions on Faraday's law, Lenz's law, and self and mutual inductance. This chapter is conceptually close to Magnetic Effects of Current, and students who study the two together, rather than as fully separate topics, tend to build a more coherent picture of how changing magnetic fields and induced currents relate to each other.

## The Modern Physics and Optics Cluster

Modern Physics and Optics together form one of the best return-on-effort areas in the entire Physics syllabus, particularly for students revising in the final weeks before the exam.

### 8. Modern Physics: Photoelectric Effect and Atomic Structure

Modern Physics, covering the photoelectric effect and atomic structure, produces a steady stream of shorter, more conceptual questions that are highly learnable in a short amount of time relative to the marks they carry. Photoelectric effect questions typically test work function, stopping potential, and threshold frequency relationships, while atomic structure questions lean on Bohr's model and the resulting energy level calculations.

### 9. Nuclear Physics

Nuclear Physics, covering radioactive decay, binding energy, and nuclear reactions, is fact-and-formula-dense but narrow in scope compared to most other chapters on this list, which makes it unusually efficient to revise. A focused single-session pass through half-life calculations and binding energy per nucleon problems tends to cover most of what actually gets tested here.

### 10. Optics: Ray and Wave Optics

Optics, split between ray optics and wave optics, tends to test standard lens and mirror formula applications alongside interference and diffraction pattern questions. It rewards careful sign convention habits more than raw formula memorisation, and sign convention mistakes, rather than not knowing the relevant formula, are the single most common source of lost marks in this chapter.

## What Makes These Ten Chapters Feel Harder Than They Are

A recurring pattern across all ten chapters is that the underlying content is more learnable than it initially feels, and the actual difficulty on exam day usually comes from a small number of specific, fixable habits rather than the chapters being inherently hard.

### The Mechanics Trap: Setup Recognition Under Pressure

In Rotational Dynamics, Circular Motion, and Gravitation, the formulas themselves are rarely the problem. What actually costs marks is failing to quickly recognise which standard setup a new question is dressed up as, especially once fatigue sets in during a full paper. The fix is not learning new formulas, it is repeated exposure to setup variation, so that a new configuration on exam day is recognised instantly as a familiar pattern rather than approached as something entirely new.

### The Electricity and Magnetism Trap: Circuit and Field Direction Errors

In Electrostatics, Current Electricity, Magnetic Effects of Current, and Electromagnetic Induction, a large share of errors come from misdrawn circuits or misjudged field directions rather than from not knowing the relevant law. Practising the habit of redrawing a complex circuit into its simplified equivalent form, or explicitly marking field direction with the right-hand rule before applying a formula, tends to produce a bigger accuracy jump than additional formula revision.

### The Modern Physics and Optics Trap: Formula Lookup Confusion

In Modern Physics, Nuclear Physics, and Optics, the most common error is applying a formula that is close to correct but subtly mismatched to the question, for example confusing a lens formula's sign convention or mixing up which energy expression applies to which specific transition. These are memory-interference problems rather than genuine gaps, and they respond specifically well to spaced, side-by-side comparison of the similar-looking formulas rather than studying each chapter in isolation.

## How to Study Each Cluster Differently

Treating all ten chapters with the same study method is one of the quieter reasons students under-perform in Physics relative to their actual effort. Each of the three clusters above responds best to a different approach.

### Mechanics: Master the Standard Setups

The most efficient way to practise Rotational Dynamics, Circular Motion, and Gravitation is to work through a chapter-mode set for each chapter separately before mixing them, since a common failure mode is confusing which standard formula applies to which setup once fatigue sets in during a full paper. Once you can solve isolated chapter sets accurately, a multi-chapter mix across all three mechanics chapters together is the right final step, because it forces you to identify the correct approach from the question text alone, which is exactly what the real exam demands.

### Electricity and Magnetism: Draw Before You Solve

Because circuit-drawing and field-direction errors are the main source of lost marks in this cluster, practise redrawing complex circuits into their simplified equivalent form, and explicitly marking field directions, as a standalone drill before attempting full numericals. A few sessions dedicated purely to this habit tend to produce a bigger accuracy jump across Electrostatics, Current Electricity, Magnetic Effects of Current, and Electromagnetic Induction than the same time spent on additional formula revision.

### Modern Physics and Optics: Build a Concept-to-Formula Map

Modern Physics, Nuclear Physics, and Optics reward students who build a single reference sheet mapping each concept to its exact formula and the specific conditions under which it applies, since the three chapters share enough surface-level similarity that cross-chapter confusion, rather than genuine lack of knowledge, is the most common source of errors. Reviewing that sheet immediately before a chapter-mode test, rather than trying to recall formulas cold, is a simple habit that measurably improves accuracy in this cluster.

## Turning This List Into a Study Plan

Knowing which ten chapters matter most is the easy part. Turning that knowledge into a study plan that actually improves your Physics score takes a few deliberate steps.

### Step 1: Audit Your Accuracy Chapter by Chapter

Before prioritising, take a chapter-mode test across each of the ten chapters listed above, or a full Physics-only paper, and record your accuracy per chapter rather than just an overall Physics score. A student scoring well overall can still be losing most of their available marks in one or two of these ten chapters specifically, and that gap will not show up anywhere in an aggregate percentage.

### Step 2: Prioritise by Weightage and Weakness

Cross-reference your chapter-level accuracy against the weightage ranking in this guide. Chapters that are both high-weightage and low-accuracy for you personally are your highest priority, since every hour spent there produces the largest expected improvement in your score. Chapters that are high-weightage but already high-accuracy need only a light maintenance pass, not further deep study, freeing up time for the chapters that actually need it. Low-weightage chapters where you are already accurate need essentially no further attention until much closer to the exam, if at all.

### Step 3: Drill and Confirm

Run focused chapter-mode drills on your highest-priority chapters, using the cluster-specific study approach described above, setup-variation drilling for Mechanics, circuit and field-direction practice for Electricity and Magnetism, and concept-to-formula mapping for Modern Physics and Optics. After each drilling session, confirm the improvement with a fresh attempt a few days later rather than assuming the work is done, and move to the next priority chapter only once the accuracy gain is confirmed with real data, not just a subjective sense that the chapter feels more familiar than it did before.

## A Sample Four-Week Physics Revision Schedule

Here is how a focused four-week Physics revision window, built around this list, might look in practice.

### Week One and Two

Run the accuracy audit across all ten chapters and begin drilling your three lowest-accuracy, highest-weightage chapters, one at a time, using the cluster-appropriate study method for each rather than switching approaches midway through a chapter. By the end of week two, you should have a clear, confirmed improvement on at least the first of these three chapters, with the second and third following close behind as drilling continues into the following days.

### Week Three

Move to the second tier of priority chapters, typically two or three more chapters from the list that scored moderately in your original audit, while running a light maintenance pass, a quick setup-recognition drill or a reference-sheet review, on the chapters you already drilled in weeks one and two, to make sure those earlier gains are actually holding rather than quietly fading as your attention moves elsewhere.

### Week Four

Attempt at least one full Physics-only paper under real timed conditions, review the results against your original audit to confirm overall progress across all ten chapters, and spend the final few days on light, low-intensity revision only, one focused pass over your formula and setup reference sheets rather than new practice. Avoid introducing any chapter you have not already covered this late in the cycle, since the risk of shaky, half-formed knowledge going into the exam usually outweighs the benefit of last-minute coverage.

## Common Mistakes Students Make With This List

A few patterns show up repeatedly among students who discover chapter-weightage data for the first time.

### Treating Mechanics as the Only Cluster That Matters

Mechanics carries the highest individual-chapter weightage, but treating it as the only cluster worth serious attention leaves real marks on the table in Electricity and Magnetism and in Modern Physics and Optics, both of which carry substantial combined weightage of their own. A balanced allocation across all three clusters, weighted by your own accuracy audit rather than by which cluster feels most familiar, produces a better outcome than concentrating almost entirely on Mechanics.

### Skipping Diagrams to Save Time

Students under time pressure often skip drawing a proper circuit diagram or force-body diagram to save a few seconds, and this is one of the most common causes of otherwise avoidable errors in Electricity and Magnetism and in Mechanics alike. A properly drawn diagram usually takes less time than the confusion it prevents, and the habit of drawing first, solving second, is worth building deliberately rather than abandoning under pressure.

### Memorising Formulas Without Understanding When They Apply

Skimming a formula sheet without practising the specific conditions under which each formula applies is rarely worth more than mastering a smaller set of formulas deeply enough that you can recognise the right one instantly from the question's setup. Depth in the ten chapters covered in this guide produces more marks, more reliably, than shallow familiarity with a wider spread of formulas you cannot confidently apply under time pressure.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the most important chapter in MHT CET Physics?

Rotational Dynamics consistently carries the highest individual weightage in MHT CET Physics, based on frequency across several years of aggregated shifts. It is closely followed by Circular Motion and Current Electricity. That said, weightage should always be cross-referenced against your own chapter-level accuracy before deciding where to focus your next study session, since a high-weightage chapter you already answer accurately needs less attention than a lower-weightage chapter you are currently getting wrong.

### Does chapter weightage in Physics change every year?

The broad pattern is stable, since it reflects the underlying syllabus emphasis rather than a specific year's paper-setting choices, but small shifts do happen from year to year. This is exactly why this ranking is built from several years of aggregated shifts rather than a single year's paper, and why revisiting the data periodically through your preparation is worth doing rather than treating this list as fixed forever.

### Should I skip low-weightage Physics chapters entirely?

No. Low-weightage does not mean zero-weightage, and a chapter that produces one question a year can still be the difference between two adjacent percentile bands. Give lower-weightage chapters a lighter revision pass, enough to answer a standard question correctly if it appears, while reserving your deepest, most repeated practice for the ten chapters covered in this guide.

### Which Physics chapters are best to study in the final two weeks before the exam?

Modern Physics, Nuclear Physics, and Optics are generally the highest-return chapters for a short revision window, since they are more conceptual and formula-driven than derivation-heavy, which makes them learnable quickly relative to the marks they carry even with only a handful of days left before the exam. Chapters you have already identified as high-weightage and low-accuracy from your own audit should still take priority over this general default, since your own data is always more reliable than a general recommendation.

### Is this Physics weightage data useful for JEE Main as well?

Not directly. While MHT CET and JEE Main share a broadly similar Physics syllabus, their question-setting patterns and relative chapter emphasis differ, partly because JEE Main draws from a CBSE-aligned syllabus document while MHT CET follows the Maharashtra state board syllabus. Use this MHT CET-specific weightage data for MHT CET preparation, and treat any crossover benefit for JEE Main as a bonus rather than a substitute for exam-specific practice.

### How is this different from the general MHT CET chapter weightage guide on this site?

This guide focuses specifically and only on Physics, going deeper into study methods tailored to the Mechanics, Electricity and Magnetism, and Modern Physics and Optics clusters than a broader guide covering all three subjects can. If you are looking for the equivalent weightage breakdown for Chemistry and Maths alongside Physics, the site's complete chapter weightage guide covers all three subjects together with a shared study-planning framework.

### I am strong in Mechanics but weak in Modern Physics. Where should I focus first?

Prioritise Modern Physics, Nuclear Physics, and Optics specifically because they respond fastest to focused study, given their more conceptual, less derivation-heavy nature, which means a few concentrated sessions can close a real gap relatively quickly. Mechanics being a strength is worth maintaining with a light periodic review rather than heavy new study time, since pouring additional hours into a chapter where your accuracy is already high produces a smaller marginal return than the same hours spent turning a genuine weakness into a strength.

### Do numerical-heavy Physics chapters need a different practice approach than conceptual ones?

Yes, and conflating the two is a common source of wasted study time. Numerical-heavy chapters like Rotational Dynamics, Circular Motion, and Current Electricity benefit most from repeated, timed practice on varied setups, since speed and setup recognition are the actual bottlenecks once the underlying formulas are known. Conceptual chapters like Modern Physics and parts of Optics benefit more from building a clear concept-to-formula map and testing recall through spaced repetition, since the bottleneck there is usually memory interference between similar-looking ideas rather than calculation speed.

### How much time should I spend on Physics relative to Chemistry and Maths?

There is no universal split that works for every student, since the right allocation depends entirely on your own chapter-level accuracy across all three subjects, not on any general rule of thumb. What is true regardless of your starting point is that within Physics itself, the ten chapters covered in this guide should receive a disproportionate share of whatever time you do allocate to the subject, since they carry a disproportionate share of the marks. Use a subject-level accuracy audit, similar to the chapter-level one described earlier in this guide, to decide the split between Physics, Chemistry, and Maths, and then use this guide specifically to decide how to spend your Physics hours once that split is set.

## Putting This Into Practice

Start with a single chapter-mode test on whichever of these ten chapters you suspect is your weakest, confirm that suspicion with real accuracy data rather than a gut feeling, and apply the cluster-appropriate study method, setup-variation drilling, circuit and field-direction practice, or concept-to-formula mapping, described in this guide. That one focused session, repeated across your remaining priority chapters over the following weeks, is the fastest way to turn this weightage list into an actual improvement in your Physics score.

Physics rewards precision more visibly than either of the other two subjects, since a single sign error or misdrawn circuit can turn an otherwise correct method into a wrong final answer. Treat this list as the starting map, not the whole journey, and let your own chapter-by-chapter accuracy data, built up through the audit-drill-confirm loop described earlier, steer the rest of your preparation from here.
